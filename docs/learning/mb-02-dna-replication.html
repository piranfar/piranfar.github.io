Replication makes two duplexes from one, and each daughter duplex keeps one old strand. Almost every exam item after that sentence is a direction, an enzyme, or a strand assignment. Learn those three lists and the questions get short.

One old strand, one new strand

Replication is semiconservative. The parental strands separate, and each is the template for a new partner. Each daughter duplex therefore contains one parental strand and one new strand.

Meselson and Stahl showed this in E. coli by labeling DNA with heavy nitrogen (15N) and then shifting the cells to light nitrogen (14N). After exactly one generation in light medium, the DNA formed a single band of intermediate density. After exactly two generations, half of the DNA was intermediate and half was light. A conservative model would have kept a fully heavy duplex and made a fully light one. The two-generation result is the one that separates semiconservative copying from the alternatives.

Who stands at the fork

Protein Job at the fork
Helicase Pulls the strands apart. It disrupts base pairs. It does not cut the backbone.
Single-strand binding protein Coats the separated DNA so the strands do not simply zip back together.
Topoisomerase Cuts and reseals DNA ahead of the fork so the unreplicated duplex does not overwind. Bacterial DNA gyrase is a type II topoisomerase.
Primase Lays down a short RNA primer. DNA polymerase cannot start a chain.
DNA polymerase Adds nucleotides to a free 3′-OH. Synthesis is 5′→3′.
Sliding clamp Holds the polymerase on the DNA. The bacterial clamp is the β clamp. The eukaryotic clamp is PCNA.
Ligase Seals a nick by making the missing phosphodiester bond. E. coli ligase uses NAD+. Eukaryotic ligase and T4 ligase use ATP.

Helicase versus topoisomerase is a standard pair of distractors. Helicase separates strands. Topoisomerase manages the twist in front of that separation. Ligase is the enzyme of the nick, not the enzyme of the primer.

Direction, primers, and the two new strands

DNA polymerase reads the template 3′→5′ and writes the new strand 5′→3′. It can add a nucleotide only onto a free 3′-OH. That is why a primer exists. In the cell the primer is RNA, made by primase. In PCR the primer is a synthetic DNA oligo, and heat does the unwinding that helicase does in the cell.

The two templates are antiparallel, and the polymerase still writes only 5′→3′. One new strand can grow continuously toward the moving fork. That is the leading strand. The other new strand is made as short pieces, Okazaki fragments, each of them still 5′→3′ and pointed away from the fork. That is the lagging strand. A common miss is to say the lagging strand is polymerized 3′→5′. It is not. The fragments face the other way, and ligase joins them later.

The newest Okazaki fragment is the short one nearest the fork. Its primer has not been removed yet.

Approximate fragment length, the range items expect:

Organism Okazaki fragments
Bacteria such as E. coli About 1,000–2,000 nucleotides
Eukaryotes About 100–200 nucleotides

Maturation is the same story in both kingdoms, with different enzymes. Primase makes the RNA primer. Polymerase extends it. The RNA is removed and the gap is filled with DNA. Ligase seals the last nick. In E. coli, DNA polymerase I removes the primer with its 5′→3′ exonuclease and fills the gap. In eukaryotes, RNase H and FEN1 do the removal work, polymerase fills, and ligase seals.

Which polymerase, on an exam

Bacterial assignments:

Enzyme What to answer
DNA polymerase III The main chromosomal replicase.
DNA polymerase I Removes RNA primers and fills the gaps. Also works in repair. It has 5′→3′ polymerase, 3′→5′ proofreading, and 5′→3′ exonuclease.
DNA polymerase II Repair and restart. Not the main replicase.

Eukaryotic assignments used on exams:

Enzyme What to answer
DNA polymerase α Works with primase. Makes the RNA–DNA starter.
DNA polymerase ε Leading strand.
DNA polymerase δ Lagging strand.
DNA polymerase γ Mitochondrial DNA.

In the cell these enzymes share a replisome, and the labor is a little more shared than a one-enzyme-one-strand sentence suggests. For the item in front of you, use the table. Pol α starts the chain. Pol ε leads. Pol δ lags. Pol γ is the mitochondrial enzyme. A choice that makes Pol α the enzyme that finishes the chromosome is swapping the starter for the replicase.

Proofreading is a direction

Replicative polymerases check the base they just added. If it is wrong, a 3′→5′ exonuclease cuts it back off and synthesis continues. That activity is proofreading. Mismatch repair is a later, separate system. It is not the same activity.

Activity Direction What it is for
Synthesis 5′→3′ Adding the next nucleotide
Proofreading 3′→5′ exonuclease Removing a base the polymerase just misincorporated
Primer removal by E. coli Pol I 5′→3′ exonuclease Clearing RNA in front of the filling polymerase

The trap is to swap the two exonuclease directions. Proofreading walks backward on the new strand. Primer removal chews forward from the 5′ side of the RNA.

The ends of a linear chromosome

Bacterial chromosomes are circular, so the fork can finish. Eukaryotic chromosomes are linear. On the lagging strand, removing the terminal RNA primer leaves a gap, and no polymerase can fill it, because there is no upstream 3′-OH to extend. That is the end-replication problem.

Telomerase solves the shortening. It is a reverse transcriptase that carries its own RNA template and extends the 3′ overhang with telomere repeats. Ordinary DNA synthesis then fills part of the complementary strand. The overhang remains, and it is part of how the end is protected. If an item says “RNA-dependent DNA polymerase at the chromosome end,” the enzyme is telomerase.

The same rules, inside a PCR tube

Step Typical temperature What replaces the cell
Denaturation 94–98°C Heat replaces helicase
Annealing Often 50–65°C, set from the primer Tm Synthetic DNA primers replace RNA primers
Extension About 72°C with Taq A thermostable polymerase replaces Pol III

Taq polymerase, from Thermus aquaticus, survives the denaturation step. Conventional Taq has little 3′→5′ proofreading, so the copied product accumulates errors. When the goal is a low error rate — cloning, for example — the reaction uses a high-fidelity polymerase that does proofread. Taq also tends to leave a single unpaired A on the 3′ end of the product. That overhang is a cloning detail, and it is fair game on a techniques item.

Say these without looking

  • Replication is semiconservative. After two generations in light nitrogen, expect half intermediate and half light DNA.
  • Helicase unwinds. Topoisomerase relieves twist ahead of the fork. Primase makes RNA primers. Ligase seals nicks.
  • Polymerase writes 5′→3′ and can start only from a 3′-OH.
  • Leading strand: one continuous chain toward the fork. Lagging strand: Okazaki fragments, each still 5′→3′.
  • E. coli Pol III replicates the chromosome. Pol I removes primers with a 5′→3′ exonuclease.
  • Exam assignment: Pol α starts, Pol ε leads, Pol δ lags, Pol γ replicates mitochondrial DNA.
  • Proofreading is 3′→5′. Primer removal by Pol I is 5′→3′.
  • PCR uses heat and DNA primers. Taq does not proofread well.

Check yourself

1. Cells grown for many generations in heavy nitrogen are switched to light nitrogen. After exactly two generations, semiconservative replication predicts which DNA?

Each daughter keeps one old strand. After two generations, half the duplexes are heavy–light and half are fully light. A heavy band plus a light band would have supported conservative copying.

2. Which enzyme separates the parental strands without cutting the phosphodiester backbone?

Helicase breaks base pairs. Topoisomerase is the enzyme that cuts the backbone, and it does so ahead of the fork to relieve twist, then reseals it.

3. DNA polymerase can add the next nucleotide only to which chemical group?

Synthesis is extension of a 3′ end, which is why a primer is required and why the chain grows 5′→3′.

4. Why is lagging-strand synthesis discontinuous?

Both new strands are made 5′→3′. One template faces the fork, so its new strand is continuous. The other template faces the other way, so its new strand is made in Okazaki fragments.

5. Which enzyme is the main replicase of the E. coli chromosome?

Pol III copies the chromosome. Pol I removes RNA primers and fills short gaps. Pol γ is the eukaryotic mitochondrial polymerase, not a bacterial enzyme.

6. In E. coli, RNA primers are removed by which activity?

Pol I uses a 5′→3′ exonuclease to clear the RNA primer, then fills with DNA. The 3′→5′ exonuclease is proofreading, a different job.

7. Proofreading by a replicative DNA polymerase is which activity?

Proofreading removes a nucleotide that was just added, so the nuclease moves 3′→5′ on the new strand. Primer removal runs the other direction.

8. On the standard exam assignment, which eukaryotic polymerase synthesizes the leading strand?

Pol ε is the leading-strand enzyme. Pol δ is assigned to the lagging strand. Pol α starts synthesis with primase. Pol γ replicates mitochondrial DNA.

9. Mitochondrial DNA is replicated by which polymerase?

Pol γ is the mitochondrial replicase. Nuclear leading and lagging strands are Pol ε and Pol δ.

10. Conventional Taq polymerase is a poor choice when the copied sequence must have the lowest possible error rate because it

Taq survives heat denaturation but does not proofread well. A high-fidelity polymerase is used when the error rate matters. Taq still extends DNA primers 5′→3′.

Where this sits

The fork drawing is an original schematic. Strand assignments for the eukaryotic polymerases follow the account used in exams and in standard texts (Pol ε on the leading strand, Pol δ on the lagging strand), with the caveat that the replisome in vivo is more cooperative than the table. Telomerase as an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase at chromosome ends is the same textbook account, as in Alberts and colleagues, Molecular Biology of the Cell. PCR enzyme behavior is the clinical-lab version of those rules, as taught in Buckingham, Molecular Diagnostics.
