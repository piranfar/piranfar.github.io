MB(ASCP) study notes · Lesson 1

DNA and RNA are polymers. The exam mostly asks you to name the parts, the bonds, and the one chemical difference that makes RNA fall apart in alkali. This lesson is that map.

NoteScope

Three pieces, two names

Every nucleotide has a nitrogenous base, a five-carbon sugar, and at least one phosphate. The sugar–phosphate chain is the backbone. The order of the bases is the information.

Molecule What it contains Examples
Base The ring only Adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine, uracil
Nucleoside Base + sugar Adenosine, deoxyadenosine, thymidine, uridine
Nucleotide Base + sugar + phosphate AMP, ATP, dATP, dTTP

The base is tied to carbon 1′ of the sugar by a glycosidic bond. Purines use nitrogen N9. Pyrimidines use nitrogen N1. The phosphate sits on carbon 5′. The next nucleotide will be attached to the free OH on carbon 3′.

Original study schematic. Carbon numbers, not artistic ring drawings, are what the items test.

Naming is part of the question. Adenine is the base, adenosine is the ribonucleoside, and AMP is the nucleotide. The prefix deoxy- means the sugar is 2′-deoxyribose, so dATP is a DNA building block. Thymidine is the usual name for the deoxyribonucleoside of thymine. Uridine is the ribonucleoside of uracil.

Purines and pyrimidines

Purines have two fused rings. Pyrimidines have one ring.

Purines Pyrimidines
Shape Six-membered ring fused to a five-membered ring One six-membered ring
Members Adenine (A), guanine (G) Cytosine (C), thymine (T), uracil (U)
Where A and G are in DNA and RNA C is in both. T is the DNA base. U is the RNA base.

The memory line is enough: purines are A and G (“pure As gold”). Everything else in this family — C, T, and U — is a pyrimidine.

A few substituents are worth being able to picture. Adenine carries an amino group. Guanine carries an amino group and a carbonyl. Thymine is uracil with a methyl group on carbon 5, which is why thymine is also called 5-methyluracil. That methyl group is the only difference the exam wants between T and U.

If the item shows rings and asks which atom bonds to the sugar Count the rings first. Two rings means a purine, and the glycosidic nitrogen is N9. One ring means a pyrimidine, and the glycosidic nitrogen is N1. A choice that swaps those two numbers is the usual distractor.

DNA and RNA

DNA RNA
Sugar 2′-deoxyribose (2′ carbon carries H) Ribose (2′ carbon carries OH)
Bases A, G, C, T A, G, C, U
Usual form Double-stranded Single-stranded, often folded
Alkali The backbone resists this particular attack The 2′-OH can cleave the backbone

The 2′-OH is the whole stability story the board wants. Under alkaline conditions that hydroxyl can attack the neighboring phosphate and cut the chain. DNA has no 2′-OH, so it does not have this path. Folding, proteins, and RNases still decide how long an RNA actually lasts in a tube. The chemical point remains: RNA is the alkali-labile nucleic acid because of the 2′-OH.

DNA polymerases are given dNTPs. RNA polymerases are given NTPs. ATP is both an energy cofactor and an RNA building block. If a stem says “the polymerase incorporates ATP,” check whether the product is RNA before you call it a trick.

Two bonds, and which direction the strands run

Hydrogen bonds pair bases across the two strands. A phosphodiester bond links nucleotides along one strand, from the 3′ carbon of one sugar to the 5′ phosphate of the next.

Pair Hydrogen bonds Where you meet it
A–T 2 DNA
A–U 2 RNA duplexes and DNA–RNA hybrids
G–C 3 DNA and RNA

G–C is the stronger pair because it has three hydrogen bonds. Base stacking along the strand stabilizes the duplex as well. The exam still treats “three versus two” as the short answer.

The strands of a duplex are antiparallel. One runs 5′→3′. Its partner runs 3′→5′. Sequence is written 5′ to 3′ unless the item says otherwise.

In double-stranded DNA, Chargaff’s rules follow from that pairing: the amount of A equals T, and the amount of G equals C. A single strand does not have to obey that equality. An item that applies Chargaff to one RNA strand is using the rule in the wrong place.

Hydrogen bonds are the cross-links. Phosphodiester bonds are the rails. Antiparallel means the 5′ ends are at opposite corners.

Melting temperature

Tm is the temperature at which half of the duplexes in a population are denatured, under the conditions you actually used. As the strands separate, absorbance at 260 nm rises. That rise is the hyperchromic effect, and it is how a melting curve is read.

What pushes Tm up, when the other variables stay put:

  • More G–C pairs, because each contributes three hydrogen bonds and favorable stacking.
  • A longer perfectly matched duplex.
  • Higher salt. Monovalent cations shield the repulsion between the phosphate backbones.

What pushes Tm down:

  • Mismatches.
  • A shorter duplex.
  • Denaturants such as formamide or urea, which is why hybridization buffers use them when the goal is a more stringent, or a more controlled, melt.

GC percent is a first estimate. Primer-design software uses nearest-neighbor parameters, because the neighbors of a pair change the stability of that pair. Two oligos with the same GC percent can still have different Tm values. For a board item that only gives GC percent, length, and “otherwise identical conditions,” choose the higher GC.

In the lab this shows up as soon as you set an annealing temperature. Primers that melt far apart, or an annealing step set far below the primer Tm, are the usual reasons a PCR becomes either weak or nonspecific. The chemistry lesson and the amplification lesson are the same fact seen from two sides.

Say these without looking

  • Purines: A and G, two rings, glycosidic bond through N9.
  • Pyrimidines: C, T, and U, one ring, glycosidic bond through N1.
  • Nucleoside = base + sugar. Nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphate.
  • DNA sugar is 2′-deoxyribose and the unique base is T. RNA sugar is ribose and the unique base is U.
  • The 2′-OH makes RNA alkali-labile.
  • A–T and A–U have two hydrogen bonds. G–C has three.
  • Phosphodiester bonds run along a strand. Hydrogen bonds pair the strands. The strands are antiparallel.
  • Tm is 50% denaturation. Higher GC, greater length, and higher salt raise it. Mismatches and formamide lower it.

Check yourself

Ten original questions. Answer all of them, then check. A miss opens a one-line reason.

1. Which bases are purines?

Adenine and guanine have two fused rings. C, T, and U are the pyrimidines.

2. A nucleoside contains which parts?

Base plus sugar is the nucleoside. Phosphate is what turns it into a nucleotide. dATP is a nucleotide, not a nucleoside.

3. Which bond covalently links neighboring nucleotides in one strand?

The phosphodiester bond is the backbone link, from 3′ of one sugar to 5′ of the next. Hydrogen bonds pair bases. The glycosidic bond holds a base on its own sugar.

4. Why is RNA more susceptible than DNA to alkaline hydrolysis?

The 2′-OH can attack the adjacent phosphate and cut the chain. DNA carries hydrogen at the 2′ carbon, so this path does not exist.

5. How many hydrogen bonds hold a G–C pair?

G–C has three hydrogen bonds. A–T and A–U have two. The bonds are not covalent.

6. Which duplex should have the highest Tm?

Length and GC content both raise Tm. Mismatches lower it. The long matched 70% GC duplex has all three factors on its side.

7. A purine is attached to C1′ of the sugar through which atom?

Purines use N9. Pyrimidines use N1. Both bonds land on C1′ of the sugar.

8. Chargaff's rules, A = T and G = C, apply directly to which molecule?

The equalities come from base pairing across two DNA strands. A single RNA strand has no partner forcing A to equal U.

9. What does Tm represent?

Tm is the midpoint of the melting transition under stated conditions: 50% of the duplex population is single-stranded. It is measured, often from the rise in A260, not assumed from a reagent label.

10. Which change tends to lower Tm?

Formamide destabilizes the duplex and lowers Tm. GC content, salt, and length of a matched duplex move Tm the other way.
