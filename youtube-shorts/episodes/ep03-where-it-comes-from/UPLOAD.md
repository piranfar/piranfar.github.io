**Title**
Where Antibiotic Resistance Comes From: One Gene, Passed Cell to Cell

**Description**
Athens, 1963. A blood culture from a patient named Temoneira grows E. coli that should have died on penicillin. The enzyme that saved it, TEM-1 beta-lactamase, is the one you met in episodes 1, 2 and Two Fates. This film asks where it comes from, and how it moves.

Not from the chromosome. The gene sits on a plasmid, a spare ring of DNA that the cell carries as a passenger. The gene is read, the protein is made, and a signal sequence sends it through the SecYEG translocon into the periplasm, where it waits by the porin. Then a sex pilus hooks a neighbour that has no enzyme, pulls it into contact, and one strand of the ring crosses, 5' end first. The neighbour builds the second strand, reads the gene, and ships its own enzyme to the periplasm. Same drug, same door: the ring breaks and the cell carries on. Within ten years of Athens, the same gene was in gonorrhoea and in the Haemophilus that gives children meningitis.

Everything shown is a real structure. B-DNA from PDB 1BNA, arrayed along the plasmid and chromosome curves. The SecYEG translocon from PDB 5AWW (OPM orientation). The 70S ribosome from PDB 4V9D. The F pilus from PDB 5LER (pED208). TEM-1 beta-lactamase, PBP1b (3VMA), AcrB (4DX5), OmpF and E. coli LPS as in the earlier episodes (CHARMM-GUI, GROMACS, MemProtMD). Rendered in Blender with Molecular Nodes.

Simplifications, stated honestly: the plasmid is shown with far fewer base pairs than a real one; transcription and translation are shown as "the gene is read" (mRNA to ribosome) without RNA polymerase; the type IV secretion system and relaxase that actually move the strand are not shown; the cytoplasm is far more crowded than drawn; the peptidoglycan mesh is stylized.

Narration: synthetic voice (Azure neural). Music: "Rain Over Kyoto Station" by The Mini Vandals (YouTube Audio Library).
